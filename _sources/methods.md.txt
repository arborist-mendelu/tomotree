# Methods for acoustic tomography on trees

Feng (2014) introduced the idea to track straight ray interceptions. Zeng (2013)
developed the technigue based on ellitical neighborhood of rays to get points 
influenced by these rays. Du (2015) used ellipses with difference eccentricities. 
Du (2018) introduced segmentation idea. However, he used segmentation by bisection 
of segments a this results in lare differences between segment lengths. For this 
reason we present variation of this method.

## Anizotropy handling

The velocity in radial direction is higher than in nonradial. We divide velocity of nonradial rays as suggested by Du (2015). Not that the denominator is less than 1 and thus $v'_N$ (fixed velocity) is bigger than $v_N$ (measured velocity).

> To obtain more accurate input data for the proposed spatial interpolation method, based on Eq.13, the velocity value of each non-radial wave path can be compensated as follows,
> $$v '_N = \frac{v_N}{-0.2\theta + 1}\tag{14}$$
> where $v_N$ is the original velocity value of a certain non-radial wave path, and $v'_N$ is the velocity value of a certain non-radial wave path after compensation.

> Although anisotropy in its most general form is mathematically awkward, Thomsen (1986) showed that these equations can be substantially simplified when approximations for ‘‘weak anisotropy’’ are made. These approximations are reasonably accurate, even for materials with moderate anisotropy such as wood. For the sake of simplicity, we have further simplified the ‘‘weak anisotropy’’ equations and assumed elliptic anisotropy (Thomsen 1986), where the angle-dependent velocity v can be written as:
> 
> $$ v=v_{\text{radial}}(1-\varepsilon\sin(\phi-\varphi)^2) \tag{10}$$
>
> where $\phi$ is the ray angle, $\varphi$ represents the azimuth of the $v_{\text{radial}}$ direction relative to the axis of the coordinate system, and $\varepsilon$ is the degree of anisotropy: 
> $$\varepsilon =\frac{v_{\text{radial}}-v_{\text{tangential}}}{v_{\text{radial}}}.$$

Du (2015) používá Taylorův rozvoj funkce publikované v Dikrallah (2006), publikaci z konference. Kromě toho, že publikace není online, nedává smysl použití Taylorova vzorce. ## EBSI

See Du (2015).

> 1. Initialization. (Generating grid graph, calculating all values of velocity rays with Eq. 2, and normalizing them.)
> 2. Compensating the values of all non-radial velocity rays with Eq. 14. $$v '_N = \frac{v_N}{-0.2\theta + 1}\tag{14}$$ 
> 3. Generating all of the affected zones with Eq. 4 and Eq. 5.
> 4. For a certain grid cell, one can record all of the elliptic zones that affect it with Eq. 6, and calculate its velocity value, using Eq. 7 and Eq. 8. 
> 5. Repeating Step 4 until every grid cell has been processed.
> 6. Visualization (reconstructing the 2D image with a certain color scale).

Citation related to the step 3:

> A ray here represents the virtual propagation path of a stress wave, and its value represents the velocity value of stress wave. Obviously, there are internal defects present on the path if the value of a certain ray is low, but the exact location of the defective part is still uncertain. Ellipses with the same eccentricity will have a greater effect on the middle portion of a ray, but the defects may be near the sensor or somewhere else. Therefore, the spatial interpolation method that uses a basic ellipse can be improved. The length is another key property of the ray. The affected zones that corresponded to shorter rays are more likely to overlap with the defective region. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the area of the affected zone that corresponds to short rays should be larger, and the weight of the affected zone that corresponds to short rays should also be strengthened. The shape of the elliptic zone can be expressed as, $$c = \frac ba \tag{3}$$ where $a$ is the long axis of the ellipse and $b$ is the short axis of the ellipse. Therefore, $c$ is similar to the eccentricity, and the controlling coefficient for a certain ray can be given as, 
> 
> $$c_{ij}=\begin{cases}1-\frac{|j-i|}{\frac N2}&|j-i|<\frac N2\cr1-\frac{N-|j-i|}{\frac N2}&|j-i|>\frac N2\cr 0.1 & |j-i|=\frac N2\end{cases}\tag{4}$$ 
> 
> where: $i \in[1, N ]$ is the sensor number of a transmitter, $j \in[1, N ]$ is the sensor number of a receiver, $N$ is the total number of sensors, and $c_{ij}$ represents the ray from sensor number $i$ to sensor number $j$. The value of $a_{ij}$ is known, so the shape of each affected zone can be controlled by $b_{ij}$: $$b_{ij} = c_{ij} \times a_{ij}.\tag{5}$$

Citation related to the step 4:

> Additionally, when a certain grid cell is affected by more than one elliptic zone, all these elliptic zones and their corresponding rays can be recorded. Then, the velocity value of the grid cell can be calculated as, $$v_{xy}=\sum_{k=1}^m w_k\times v_k\tag{7}$$ where $v_{xy}$ is the estimated velocity value of a certain grid cell, $v_k$ is the velocity value of a certain ray that affects the specific grid cell, $w_k$ is weighting coefficient, and $m$ is the total number of rays that affect the specific grid cell, simultaneously. Considering that elliptical zones that correspond to short rays should weigh more, $w_k$ can be given as, $$w_k = \frac {1}{l_k}\Biggm/ \sum_{z=1}^m \frac{1}{l_z}\tag{8}$$ where $l_z$ is the length of a certain ray.




## ERSM

Improvement of Du (2018).

1. Choose typical size of the segment $l_0$. One half of the shortest ray is suggested length.
1. Split each ray into segments of equal size. The number of segments is the integer part of the quotient $l/l_0$ where $l$ is the legth of the ray. **This is the change from Du (2018)**
1. For each segment consider the ellipse with major axis at the segment and the eccentricity given. Find all segments intercepting this ellipse and set the speed of this segment as a mean value of the speeds of segments through the ellipse.
2. Set the cell velcities using spatial interpolation by segmented ellipe (SISE) as described in Du (2018).


## SIRT method

Sluits and Vorst (1990) describe general SIRT methods. It $b_i$ is the time of fly of the $i$-th ray, $a_{ij}$ is the length of the intersection of the $i$-th ray with the $j$-th cell and $x_j$ is the slowness (reciprocal value of velocity) in the $j$-th cell, then we have 
$$b_i = \sum a_{ij} x_j$$
or in matrix form
$$b = Ax,$$
where $b$ and $x$ are column matrices of times and slownesses respectively and $A$ is the matrix of segment lengths. In general, this linear system with unknowns $x$ does not have solution. Thus we have to find vector $x$ which is close to the solution in the sense that the euclidean norm $$||b-Ax||$$ of the difference between the left and right hand side is minimal.

Simultaneous iterative reconstruction technique (SIRT) is the method to solve this least square problem iteratively. 
Sluis and Vorst (1990) present a class of iterative formulas 

$$x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}+\omega P A^T Q (b-Ax),\tag{*}$$

where $P=\mathrm{diag}(1/\gamma_j)$, $Q=\mathrm{diag}(1/\rho_j)$ and
$$\gamma_j=\sum_i |a_{ij}|^\alpha, \quad 
\rho_j=\sum_k |a_{ij}|^{2-\alpha}, \quad
0\leq \alpha\leq 2, \quad 0<\omega<2.$$
Common choices are $\omega =1$ and either $\alpha = 0$ or $\alpha =1$. The scheme (*) is convergent by Sluis and Vorst (1990), Theorem 4.10.

### Fixing reaction time in SIRT method

> Maurer (2006): Finally, we highlight a common problem in acoustic wood
> tomography. Owing to inherent instrumental limitations, it is often difficult
> or impossible to determine precisely the difference between the origin time of
> the seismic pulse and the time of the first sample of the digitized seismogram.
> This time difference is generally unknown, but it usually remains constant
> during an experiment. Consequently, the origin time to may be
> included as a further unknown in the tomographic inversion procedure by modifying Eq. (1):
>
> $$ t_{ij}=\sum_{\text{cells}}l_{ijk}s_k+t_0 \tag{5}$$
> 
> As shown by Maurer (1996), it is only necessary to append a column with values
> of 1 to matrix $G$ to solve the inverse problem related to Eq. (5). The
> solution vector $s$ will then include adjustments not only to the slowness of
> individual cells, but also to the origin time $t_0$.
